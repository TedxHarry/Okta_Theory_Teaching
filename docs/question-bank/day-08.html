Day 8: Questions

Federation and participant roles

Q01. Explain federation

Question: Maya signs in to Salesforce, a business application, through Okta, her company’s identity service. Their configured SAML connection exchanges identity information under agreed validation rules. Why can Salesforce rely on that information without receiving Maya’s password?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

This is federated sign-in: the application relies on identity information from another service under a configured trust relationship. SAML, Security Assertion Markup Language, supplies a standard for that exchange.

Okta performs the applicable checks and issues information Salesforce must validate. Trust does not mean accepting any message claiming to be from Okta.

Keep both jobs in view. The identity-service result and the application's acceptance are separate outcomes.

Q02. Assign the roles

Question: In a SAML connection, Okta checks Maya’s identity and issues sign-in information; Salesforce validates it and decides application entry; Maya’s browser carries messages between them. Which system is the identity provider (IdP), which is the service provider (SP), and what authority does the browser have?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Okta is the IdP supplying identity information. Salesforce is the SP receiving and validating it. The browser carries the exchange; it does not decide whether Maya should be admitted.

The application still identifies its account and controls entry and permissions. These roles describe the stated connection rather than permanent labels for every product.

Name each participant's responsibility before interpreting its result. Delivery by the browser cannot replace the SP's acceptance evidence.

Q03. What is not sent?

Question: Active Directory (AD) accepts Maya’s password during an earlier credential check. Okta then provides SAML identity information to Salesforce through her browser. In this configured federation flow, does Salesforce receive that AD password?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. It receives an assertion about identity and authentication, not Maya's directory password.

An assertion is a set of statements the application evaluates under its trusted connection. The earlier credential check and the later message exchange are different steps.

Knowing AD accepted a password does not show Salesforce accepted the assertion. Follow the received information and validation result instead of treating the federation message as another copy of the password.

Requests, responses, and assertions

Q04. Three objects, three jobs

Question: Salesforce starts a SAML sign-in by sending an AuthnRequest through the browser to Okta. After applicable checks, Okta returns a successful SAML response containing an assertion. How do these three objects differ in purpose?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Start with what Salesforce needs: authentication information about the person trying to enter. Its authentication request asks Okta to provide that information under the connection's requirements.

Okta sends a SAML response as its reply. In this successful-response flow, the response contains an assertion with statements about the user and authentication, together with conditions on their use.

The request asks, the response replies, and the assertion supplies statements for Salesforce to check. Receiving a successful reply does not finish Salesforce's work. It still needs to validate the information and identify the intended application account.

Q05. Protocol success and SP rejection

Question: Okta returns a SAML response with protocol-level success, but Salesforce reports that the contained assertion fails an application validation requirement. Can both results be correct, and does the success force Salesforce to admit Maya?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Both can be correct. The response status does not require the receiving application to accept information that fails its checks.

Salesforce compares the message with the trusted connection, intended audience, validity, and identity requirements. A plausible username cannot override another failed condition.

Keep the response result and receiving decision separate. The reply gives the application material to evaluate, not permission to ignore its validation contract.

Q06. A readable claim

Question: A support analyst reads Maya’s name in a SAML assertion, a statement supplied for federated sign-in. No signature verification, intended-audience, time, or acceptance result is supplied. What remains unknown before the application can trust that statement?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It still needs to establish the required signed content's origin and integrity, intended use, and applicable conditions. Reading a claim does not prove those properties.

Use the connection's trusted configuration and actual validation evidence. The visible name helps locate content, but it cannot show who produced it or whether this attempt may use it.

Distinguish readable information from acceptable proof. The receiving system must make its own validation decision.

Follow the browser delivery

Q07. Trace an SP-initiated flow

Question: Maya begins at Salesforce, a business application configured to use Okta for SAML identity checks. Salesforce starts by sending an authentication request through her browser. Describe the simplified service-provider-initiated journey and where the application decides entry.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The browser carries Salesforce's request to Okta. After the applicable checks, Okta supplies a response that the browser delivers back to Salesforce.

Salesforce validates the information, matches the intended account, and decides access. It is SP-initiated because the service provider began the exchange.

That describes the intended route, not a completed incident trace. Actual evidence is needed to establish which stages occurred for the identified attempt.

Q08. Who carries the final message?

Question: Maya’s SAML sign-in uses browser HTTP POST delivery from Okta to Salesforce. Okta can return an HTML form containing the encoded response in a hidden SAMLResponse field. How does the message travel, and must Maya type the assertion herself?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Maya normally does not type the SAML response. In this example, Okta provides a web page with a form. A hidden field named SAMLResponse holds the response, and the browser submits the form to the application's receiving address using HTTP POST, often automatically.

That explains how information travels back even though Maya does not fill in a visible message box. The browser carries the response; it does not decide whether the application should trust it.

The application must still validate what arrives and match the user. A completed form submission establishes delivery, not successful validation, account creation, or permission to perform every action.

Q09. What the ACS does

Question: Salesforce receives a SAML sign-in response at its Assertion Consumer Service (ACS), whose configured endpoint is the receiving address. Delivery is confirmed, but application validation and account matching are not supplied. What does reaching the ACS establish?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It establishes where the response arrived. The ACS receives it for the application's sign-in processing.

Arrival does not establish validation or identification of the intended account. The message can reach the right address while failing a different acceptance requirement.

Keep receiving and accepting separate. Obtain the relevant application results before reporting entry, rather than make a delivery observation supply every later outcome.

Q10. POST is not account creation

Question: Maya already has an active Salesforce account. In her configured SAML sign-in, the browser POSTs a response to the application’s ACS receiving endpoint; no account creation occurs in this stated example. Does the HTTP POST itself prove a new account was created?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. POST describes a message operation. Here its purpose is delivering sign-in information for validation, not a creation result.

The question supplies an existing account and no creation in the journey. Preserve those facts instead of inferring another operation from the method.

Some applications have separately supported sign-in-time account behavior, but that needs configuration and evidence. The delivery method alone cannot establish it.

Q11. Where the flow starts

Question: A SAML application can support launching from the identity provider, such as Okta, or starting at the service provider, such as Salesforce. How do IdP-initiated and SP-initiated starts differ, and does either remove application validation?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

An IdP-initiated launch starts at the identity provider, rather than with the same authentication request from the application. An SP-initiated journey starts at the service provider.

Support and validation details depend on the integration. A different starting point does not remove the application's obligation to check the response.

Identify the actual launch before interpreting the exchange. Neither route is a reason to ignore audience, signing trust, or account matching.

Q12. Every prompt every time?

Question: A simplified SAML flow includes the stage “Okta performs applicable authentication checks.” No prompt-by-prompt trace or existing-session context is supplied. Does that description prove every application launch repeats a password and every additional factor?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The interaction depends on the applicable requirements and existing context. A logical stage does not prescribe the same screens for every launch.

Read the evidence for the actual attempt rather than invent repeated proof requests. Existing authentication may affect which interactions occur.

The receiving application still validates the returned information. The absence of a particular prompt is not, by itself, an acceptance result or a policy defect.

Configuration and identifiers

Q13. What metadata communicates

Question: Okta and a business application use SAML metadata to communicate identifiers, endpoints, and signing certificates for their connection. A metadata document is present, but no actual sign-in message or acceptance result is supplied. What does metadata contribute?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It communicates connection details forming the intended agreement. Some integrations exchange a document; others configure the relevant values separately.

The actual message still has to satisfy that agreement. Metadata presence does not show what a response contained or whether the application accepted it.

Keep expected configuration beside observed values. That comparison gives the document practical meaning without turning it into proof of a successful attempt.

Q14. Identifier or receiving address?

Question: A SAML connection has an entity ID that looks like a web URL and a separate ACS receiving URL. No rule says they are the same address. Must the entity ID be the endpoint to which the browser delivers the response?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Entity ID identifies a SAML participant. Its URL-like shape does not automatically make it a receiving endpoint.

The ACS URL answers where the response is delivered. For the stated single-connection model, the service-provider identifier supplies the expected audience, which describes the intended service.

Read each value by its job. Similar-looking strings can identify different relationships in the same exchange.

Q15. Four values with different jobs

Question: A SAML sign-in connection records issuer, audience, ACS, and NameID. Match them to these questions: who claims to issue the information, which service may use it, where the response is received, and which user it describes under the chosen matching rule.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Give each value one question to answer. Issuer asks, 'Who issued this information?' Audience asks, 'Which service may use this assertion?' ACS asks, 'Where is the response delivered?' NameID asks, 'Which user does it describe under this connection's matching rule?'

For a company application, the receiving address, application identifier and user identifier serve different purposes even if some look like web or email addresses. Their appearance does not make them interchangeable.

Compare each observed value with the corresponding approved setting. A correct user identifier cannot fix an assertion intended for another service, and a correct delivery address cannot establish the user match.

Q16. Right address, wrong audience

Question: Maya’s SAML response reaches Salesforce’s production ACS. Its assertion instead names the test service as audience, where production is expected. Audience identifies which service may use the assertion. Can delivery be correct while that separate acceptance condition is wrong?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Yes, those facts can occur together. The browser can deliver the response to production's ACS while the assertion inside names the test service as its audience.

The address answers where the response went. The audience answers which service may use the assertion. Reaching the right address does not rewrite the value inside it or make that value acceptable.

Compare the audience actually sent with production's expected audience, then inspect the application's validation report for this attempt. That report is needed to establish whether the mismatch caused this rejection; successful delivery alone cannot settle it.

Q17. The chosen matching rule

Question: A company configures Salesforce to match a SAML NameID against the application username. Another deployment may choose a separate Federation ID. Is username matching universal, and what should be checked before comparing a user value?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It is not universal. Inspect the chosen rule for the actual connection. Federation ID is a separate user identifier the application can use for federated matching.

NameID carries a value, but the application's configuration determines which record field it must match. Email-like text is not automatically the correct identifier.

Keep the intended matching rule beside the observed values. Otherwise a comparison can answer the wrong identity question.

Q18. Keep test and production separate

Question: Support investigates a production SAML failure but has expected settings from a test application and a response whose originating connection is unverified. Why confirm the instance before comparing issuer, audience, receiving address, or user mapping?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A value correct for test can be wrong for production. The expected agreement and observed message must belong to the intended connection.

First establish the instance and relevant attempt. Then compare the fields according to their purposes and use validation evidence.

Mixing environments can create a misleading diagnosis even when individual settings are valid somewhere. Configuration context is part of the evidence, not a detail to add after choosing the cause.

Read the XML evidence

Q19. Read a named element

Question: A selected fictional SAML XML element is <saml:NameID>maya.rao@northbridge.example</saml:NameID>. XML uses opening and closing tags to surround values. What user identifier does this element claim, and what does reading it leave unverified?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Its value is maya.rao@northbridge.example. The tags name the element as NameID in the declared SAML vocabulary.

Compare that claim with the connection's chosen account-matching rule and approved identifier. Reading the text does not establish that the full message was authentic or accepted.

Content inspection is useful for locating values. It is not the same job as application validation.

Q20. What the prefix means

Question: A SAML XML excerpt declares xmlns:saml and uses a tag named saml:NameID with a user value inside it. Does the saml: prefix identify the user, or what vocabulary role does it serve?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It refers to the declared namespace, the vocabulary to which the element name belongs. The NameID value, not the prefix, supplies the user identifier.

You do not need to memorize the namespace address to read the selected field. Identify the named element and the text it contains.

Structure helps interpret the message, while the actual matching and validation evidence establish whether the application can use it.

Q21. Find a visible audience discrepancy

Question: For a production SAML connection, expected audience is https://app.example/entity/prod. A selected assertion shows https://app.example/entity/test, while issuer and NameID match their expected text. No validation report is supplied. What visible discrepancy exists, and is its causal role already proven?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The audience names test where production is expected. That is a visible mismatch worthy of investigation.

The excerpt does not establish the application's rejection reason or all other checks. Obtain the correlated validation report before making that causal claim.

Inspection and confirmed diagnosis are distinct steps. The selected fields can locate a problem to check without supplying the complete outcome of the attempt.

Q22. An excerpt is not a full assertion

Question: Support receives an intentionally shortened SAML assertion excerpt containing only issuer, NameID, and audience. Signature material and validity fields are omitted from the excerpt, and the full message is not shown. Does this prove the full response lacked them?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Omission from selected evidence is not confirmed absence from the complete message. The excerpt intentionally limits what can be inspected.

Signature and time conclusions need the full relevant content and validation result. Do not declare a missing signature merely because the shortened view leaves it out.

State what the excerpt establishes and request the evidence required for the remaining checks. That preserves its value without expanding its scope.

Validation and trust

Q23. Readable versus validated

Question: A support analyst decodes a SAML assertion and sees readable identity fields plus a signature element. No trusted-key verification result is supplied. Does seeing those contents establish that the application successfully verified the signature?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Decoding reveals content. Seeing signature material is not the cryptographic verification result.

The application must check the required signed content using its configured trusted signing information. Then it evaluates the other acceptance conditions too.

Do not turn visible text into a completed validation claim. It helps locate fields, while actual verification evidence establishes whether the receiver may rely on them.

Q24. Private key and certificate

Question: A SAML identity provider signs information using a private signing key. The application is configured with trusted certificate information for verification. What different jobs do the private key and signing certificate perform, and is either the user’s password?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The private signing key is a cryptographic secret used to create the digital signature. The signing certificate supplies corresponding public-key information that the application can use to verify the required signed content.

This is a different job from checking a user's password. The application checks the SAML signature using its trusted signing configuration; it does not need the user's AD password to perform that check.

The important result is successful verification under the intended trust configuration. Merely finding a certificate or signature element does not establish that result, and generating a certificate does not automatically make the application trust it.

Q25. Signing versus encryption

Question: A SAML assertion is signed but readable when inspected. Encryption is not assumed. Does readability make signing ineffective, and how do signature verification and encryption address different concerns?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A signed message can be readable. Signing supports checking origin and integrity of the signed content; encryption restricts reading without the required decryption key.

They have different purposes. Readable content is not automatically unsigned or invalid, and encryption alone would not settle every acceptance requirement.

Inspect the supported connection rules and actual verification results. The visible form of the message does not establish either its trust or intended use.

Q26. A valid signature is not every check

Question: The receiving SAML application reports that required signature verification passed. No audience or identity-matching result is supplied. Must the assertion also be intended for this application and match the right account?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Those results are not established by the signature alone. Authentic signed content can still name another audience or identify a user differently from the application's chosen rule.

Inspect the separate checks against the approved connection. Audience identifies intended use; matching identifies the local account.

Passing one important condition is progress, not a substitute for the rest. Keep each validation outcome attached to the question it answers.

Q27. Time conditions

Question: A SAML application evaluates assertion conditions such as NotBefore and NotOnOrAfter. A report names a validity rejection but gives no detailed clock or setting analysis. What do those fields constrain, and does the rejection alone identify the exact time defect?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

They constrain when the assertion may be used. The receiver must determine whether it is usable at the checked point under the applicable conditions.

A generic time rejection does not identify which clock or setting is wrong. Obtain the relevant full message and validation evidence before choosing a correction.

Understand the purpose without inventing a precise cause. Time is one acceptance boundary, not the entire sign-in decision.

Q28. Correlation and replay

Question: A SAML service provider receives identity information for a request-based sign-in. The flow requires checking that the response belongs to the expected request and was not improperly reused. Why are these checks needed in addition to a plausible user identifier?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The response needs to fit this sign-in attempt, not merely look like a valid message from somewhere in the past. Where required, request correlation connects it to the authentication request the application expects.

Replay means presenting previously issued information again. Replay checks prevent improper reuse. Otherwise, someone could try to reuse earlier signed information instead of completing the exchange required now. A valid signature alone does not establish that this reuse is acceptable.

Check the request relationship, applicable validity conditions and replay result for the actual flow. An IdP-initiated launch starts differently, so do not invent a preceding SP request when that flow does not have one.

Investigate an audience rejection

Q29. What establishes the audience cause?

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Production expects the audience /entity/prod, but the full assertion names /entity/test; the correlated application report rejects that mismatch. How does the expected configuration, full-message value, and rejection report establish more than a selected field difference alone?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

All three concern the same connection and attempt and point to the same failed audience requirement. The report connects the mismatch to the actual refusal.

A shortened excerpt alone would only locate a discrepancy to investigate. The correlated application evidence now establishes its causal role.

Focus correction on the emitted audience for the intended agreement. Preserve any other accepted results instead of replacing the diagnosis with an unrelated credential explanation.

Q30. Keep accepted checks

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Production expects the audience /entity/prod, but the full assertion names /entity/test; the correlated application report rejects that mismatch. Issuer, required signature, and validity pass; her active target account has the correct username matching NameID. Would changing that username repair the failed audience requirement?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The identifier already agrees with the supplied matching requirement. The defect concerns which service the assertion is intended for.

Inspect the connection value producing the test audience for this production attempt. Changing a correct user field would add another change without repairing the mismatch.

The accepted results come from validation evidence, not mere readable fields. Keep those findings while addressing the specific failed condition.

Q31. Do not bypass the failed check

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Production expects the audience /entity/prod, but the full assertion names /entity/test; the correlated application report rejects that mismatch. Would turning off audience validation be a correction that satisfies the intended production agreement?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It would remove the check that caught the mismatch, not bring the message into agreement. The intended result requires the appropriate production audience.

Compare approved settings on both sides and identify why the wrong value was emitted. Review shared integration scope before applying the justified change.

Correct the defective relationship and verify a fresh attempt. Accepting inappropriate proof is not the same outcome as producing correct proof.

Q32. A shared connection change

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. An audience-setting correction is proposed after a production/test mismatch, and other users share that integration. Why review more than Maya’s account before applying the change?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The configuration can affect everyone using the shared connection. One person's report does not make the audience setting private to them.

Review the intended instance, approved value, affected users, and verification plan. Then check fresh messages and acceptance under the corrected agreement.

The ticket identifies where the problem was noticed; the configuration identifies the wider change scope. Both belong in the recommendation.

Q33. Saved is not verified

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. After a confirmed test-versus-production audience defect, support saves a proposed setting correction. No new message or application result is supplied. What evidence would verify the fix rather than just the action?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A fresh attempt should emit the expected production audience, receive application acceptance, and identify Maya's intended account.

Saving a setting establishes an action, not what the next message contained or how Salesforce treated it. An old identity-service success cannot verify a new attempt.

Specify those follow-up checks and leave them unverified until supplied. The proposal becomes a demonstrated correction only through its actual result.

Q34. Repair a closure note

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Production expects the audience /entity/prod, but the full assertion names /entity/test; the correlated application report rejects that mismatch. Okta records success, and Maya’s active account exists with NameID matching the approved username. No correction is supplied. Improve: “Okta succeeded, so Salesforce worked; her account must be missing.”

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Try: “Okta recorded success for the checked attempt, but Salesforce rejected the assertion because its audience named test rather than production. Maya's intended account exists and has the expected identifier. The responsible connection settings need correction and fresh verification.”

That preserves both services' outcomes and the known account facts.

Do not invent a missing record or completed fix. The supplied discrepancy is the rejected audience, not failed credentials or absence of the application account.

Investigate account matching

Q35. A demonstrated user-matching discrepancy

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. This connection matches NameID against username. The approved account username is maya.rao@app.example, but the assertion carries maya.old@app.example. Audience and required validation pass; the application reports no matching user. Where should support investigate?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Follow the matching rule stated in this question: NameID must match the approved application username. The supplied NameID is outdated, and the application reports no matching user. The audience and required validation checks have already passed.

That places the next investigation at user identification. Compare the NameID actually delivered with the approved username and linked application record, then trace the mapping that supplied it. Changing the audience would address a check that already passed.

Resolve which existing account this sign-in should identify before considering account creation. Another account could leave the incorrect identifier in place and add confusion about which record belongs to the person.

Q36. Do not create another account first

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Her intended account exists as maya.rao@app.example, but NameID sends maya.old@app.example under username matching. The receiver rejects the match and other required checks pass. Why investigate the identifier and association before creating another account?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The supplied defect concerns the value used to find the intended existing account. Another creation could introduce a duplicate instead of repairing that relationship.

Compare the target, chosen matching rule, and configured versus delivered NameID. Establish the approved correction from that evidence.

The goal is the right account association, not a new record that accommodates an inaccurate identifier. No new-account requirement is supplied here.

Q37. Federation ID variation

Question: A Salesforce SAML connection explicitly matches NameID to Federation ID, a separate federated user identifier. Support compares the assertion only with the target username and has not read Federation ID. Does that comparison establish a correct account match?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Username is not the chosen matching field in this scenario. Inspect the configured Federation ID and actual NameID for the intended record.

The connection supplies the meaning of the value. Familiar names or email-like appearance cannot replace that agreement.

Keep expected identity configuration beside the observed message. A comparison against the wrong field can look reassuring while leaving the relevant match unanswered.

Account lifecycle and permissions

Q38. SAML and JIT

Question: A business application receives a SAML assertion for sign-in. Its account-management capabilities and just-in-time provisioning settings are unknown. Does receiving the assertion guarantee account creation, and what would JIT require?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. SAML supplies sign-in information, not an automatic creation guarantee. Just-in-time provisioning is separately supported and configured behavior that can create or update an account during sign-in.

Inspect the particular application and operation evidence before making an account claim. A target account may already exist or need another preparation process.

Authentication and account management remain separate concerns even when a configured journey connects them.

Q39. Accepted sign-in, denied action

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. The application accepts the response and selects her intended account. A later business action is denied, but its approval and role requirements are unknown. Does that denial automatically demonstrate an audience defect?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Entry is supplied as successful. Investigate whether the business action is approved and what permission the application actually grants.

Authentication and account identification do not make every action available. Preserve the accepted sign-in instead of reopening an audience defect without evidence.

Locate the new problem at the action boundary. The required role and denial evidence will distinguish an intended restriction from a permission discrepancy.

Q40. Keep independent attempts separate

Question: Support reviews two independent Salesforce attempts for Maya. The earlier report leaves assignment and account existence uninspected. A separate later case supplies an assignment and active account but no evidence linking those facts to the earlier point. Do they retroactively resolve the earlier unknowns?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The later facts belong to their stated case and time. They do not reconstruct the earlier record state or supply its missing outcomes.

Use supported correlation and relevant historical evidence before extending a finding to another attempt. A familiar person and application do not make every incident one record.

The independent case can support its own investigation while the earlier questions remain open. Preserve both scopes rather than borrow later certainty.

Compare, verify, and explain

Q41. Three things to compare

Question: Support investigates a SAML connection between Okta and a business application. It has provider settings, receiver settings, and a captured message, but their production/test context has not been confirmed. What should be compared and in what context?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

First establish that the three sources describe the intended connection and attempt. Then compare issuer, intended audience, ACS receiving address, and user identifier according to their jobs.

The provider setting does not prove the actual message value, and receiver configuration supplies what it expects to validate.

Keep all three together. That connects approved behavior to the emitted information and avoids explaining one instance with settings from another.

Q42. A new certificate is not new trust

Question: Okta creates a new SAML signing certificate for a Salesforce integration. The active signing choice, receiver’s trusted certificate, and supported transition process have not been checked. Does generation alone prove Salesforce trusts the new relationship?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Creating public-key information does not itself update or verify receiving trust. Inspect which signing key is active and which information Salesforce trusts.

Coordinate the supported transition before activation, then verify a fresh response and acceptance. The certificate is connection trust information, not the user's password.

Generation is a configuration step. The agreement and actual verification result establish whether the changed signing relationship works.

Q43. An old session after a signing change

Question: Maya continues using an existing Salesforce session after a proposed SAML certificate change. No fresh sign-in response under the new signing choice is supplied. Does continued use of that old session verify the changed signing relationship?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The established application session may continue without exercising a new SAML exchange. Its accepted request does not supply a fresh signature-validation result.

Verify a new response and application acceptance under the intended connection. Distinguish that test from continued use through an old session.

The session observation remains valid evidence, but it answers a different question. A trust change needs verification that actually uses the changed path.

Q44. Explain the investigation to Maya

Question: Maya uses Okta, an identity service, for SAML sign-in to Salesforce, a separate business application. Production expects the audience /entity/prod, but the full assertion names /entity/test; the correlated application report rejects that mismatch. Her applicable Okta checks succeed and her active Salesforce account exists. No corrected attempt is supplied. Explain the failure and required follow-up to Maya in friendly language.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

You could tell Maya: 'You completed the Okta checks, but Salesforce also checks the information it receives. For the attempt we reviewed, that information named the test Salesforce connection even though you were entering production. Salesforce rejected that mismatch. Your intended account exists and is active.'

Then explain what happens next: 'We need to correct the connection value responsible for that mismatch, after checking who shares the setting. A fresh sign-in must then show the production audience and successful entry into your intended account before we call the correction verified.'

This explains the known failure without blaming Maya's password or promising a repair that has not yet been tested. Any permissions needed after entry remain a separate application question.
