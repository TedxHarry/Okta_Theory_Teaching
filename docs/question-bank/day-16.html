Day 16: Questions

Choose a question and useful boundary cases

Q01. A small first experiment

Question: A learner plans an authorized identity training exercise. They can ask whether Finance AND Employee selects the intended group members, or attempt to “make all access work” across accounts, sign-in, and permissions. Why is the narrower first question easier to investigate?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It identifies one relationship and a result you can predict. Membership can be checked against inputs and rule processing without changing several account and access boundaries at once.

A small experiment is useful when its conclusion stays small. Correct membership teaches something concrete even though it does not establish account creation, application entry, or permissions. Start with the defined comparison, then add another relationship as a separate question.

Q02. Predict the four profiles

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. Before observing rule processing, who should satisfy the intended logical condition, and how should this prediction be labeled?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Only Casey meets both conditions. Morgan meets neither, Riley meets Finance only, and Taylor meets Employee only. AND requires both parts to be true.

These are predicted results, not observed memberships. Actual testing still needs the intended fields, applicable user states, active rule, exclusions, and processing evidence. Write the expectation first so later results can be compared with the requirement instead of deciding what should happen after seeing the screen.

Q03. Predict the broader mistake

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. For a paper comparison only, which profiles would satisfy Finance OR Employee instead, and what does that reveal?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Casey, Riley, and Taylor satisfy OR; Morgan does not. OR requires at least one condition, while AND requires both.

Riley and Taylor expose the broader population because each meets just one part. Casey and Morgan have the same predictions under both expressions. This is a hypothetical comparison, not a direction to activate the wrong rule. It helps explain which profiles can reveal an error in the intended logic.

Q04. Why Casey and Morgan are insufficient

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. The learner checks only Casey included and Morgan excluded. Why could those two results fail to detect a mistaken Finance OR Employee condition?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Casey meets both parts, so either AND or OR would include Casey. Morgan meets neither, so either expression would exclude Morgan. Those two results cannot tell you which expression is operating.

Riley and Taylor make the difference visible. Riley meets Finance without Employee; Taylor meets Employee without Finance. AND should exclude each, while OR would include each.

Include those one-condition cases when checking the intended comparison. A test can produce the expected result for two people while still missing the very mistake it needs to detect.

Q05. Do not rewrite a contractor

Question: A training rule requires Finance AND Employee, and Riley is deliberately a Finance contractor with no approved exception. A learner proposes changing workerType to Employee so Riley appears in the group. Why would that undermine the intended test?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Riley should fail the normal condition. Changing the classification would misrepresent the training requirement and remove a useful boundary case.

If an exception exercise is wanted later, define its own approval, scope, owner, and end condition. Classification and access exceptions answer different questions. Test the agreed condition against the supplied person rather than changing the person until the rule produces the desired membership.

Identify environment, inputs, and processing

Q06. A familiar browser tab

Question: A learner is ready to make an approved training change and sees an Okta browser tab with a familiar application name. The organization and integration instance have not been verified. What environment facts are needed before treating it as the intended destination?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Identify the training organization, application integration instance, and target environment if one is involved. Confirm who may change them and who can restore the prior state.

A familiar tab or name is not environment evidence. The written plan can be completed without a tenant; execution needs the authorized training destination and synthetic identities. Naming those facts keeps the experiment's observations tied to the place where its actions are actually permitted.

Q07. Synthetic data versus an HR import

Question: In an authorized training organization, a learner enters synthetic department and workerType values directly into Okta. A group evaluation produces the predicted membership. No Workday HR import occurs. Does the experiment verify Workday sourcing as well as membership?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. It tests the central-profile input and membership relationship using entered training data. It does not supply an HR source operation or import result.

Label that boundary clearly. If the environment has an existing source connection, check field ownership and supported update behavior before editing sourced values. Success from one setup should not be described as verification of a different architecture that the experiment never exercised.

Q08. Check the actual fields

Question: An approved group experiment uses department Finance AND workerType Employee. The written plan lists those fields, but the training schema, stored profiles, and expression references are unchecked. Why inspect all three before interpreting membership?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Check three things separately. The schema defines whether the intended fields exist. The stored profiles show what values those fields actually hold. The rule references show which fields the comparison uses.

Writing department and workerType in a plan does not establish that the organization has the intended definitions, data or references. A missing value or a different field reference could otherwise look like a comparison problem.

Inspect those inputs before interpreting membership. Then compare the rule's actual evaluation and result with the prediction, rather than choosing a cause from the display alone.

Q09. Active-user context

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. The exercise uses attribute-rule behavior applicable to Active users and specifies no different status condition. Why record all four users’ actual account states before attributing missing membership to department logic?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

An inactive profile can make absence misleading when the evaluation is scoped to Active users. Record state so the department and classification comparison is tested within the intended processing context.

Still check rule state, exclusions, evaluation, and stored membership. Matching profile values alone do not establish the result. Holding the relevant conditions clear helps the learner isolate the comparison instead of diagnosing a logic defect from an unexplained absence.

Q10. Saved rule, unchanged members

Question: A learner saves the intended Finance AND Employee group rule. It is initially Inactive, and no evaluation result is supplied. Membership has not changed. What should be checked before diagnosing an incorrect expression?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The saved rule is Inactive, so the unchanged membership is not yet evidence that the intended comparison ran and failed. Saving and evaluating are different stages.

Check the intended activation and relevant evaluation under the approved experiment, along with the actual condition and inputs. Only then can the membership result test what the rule does.

Keep the observations separate: the expression was saved, the mechanism has not been shown to evaluate it, and membership is unchanged. That explains the next check without inventing an incorrect expression.

Q11. Four planned people, wider rule scope

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. Activating the rule can evaluate matching profiles across the training organization, and the dedicated group has no app assignments. Does naming four people in the plan limit actual evaluation to only them?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The written population and actual configuration scope are different. Review other profiles the condition could include and preview the planned cases before activation.

The group has no application assignments here, keeping the outcome focused on membership. That does not make its evaluation scope four named users. Understand both the test cases and wider condition so observations and possible effects are interpreted within the actual organization.

Q12. The starting state

Question: An authorized experiment will add a group-based application assignment for a synthetic user. Existing profiles, memberships, direct assignments, and target associations are unknown. What starting evidence would help distinguish the new experiment’s effects from relationships already present?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Record what exists before the new action: relevant profile values, memberships, assignment sources and target-account associations. That starting state gives you something concrete to compare with the later result.

If the person already had a direct application assignment, seeing the application afterward would not show that the new group supplied it. The result might be real while the explanation of its source is wrong.

Keep the before-and-after records tied to the intended instance and change. They help distinguish a relationship introduced by the experiment from one it simply encountered.

Compare predictions with observed results

Q13. Prediction before action

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. The authorized membership experiment has not run. What should the learner write before acting so predictions, required evidence, and later observations remain distinct?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Record each profile's inputs, the intended Finance AND Employee condition, and the expected membership. Identify the rule-state and processing evidence needed to interpret actual results.

That gives the experiment a clear question before the interface supplies an answer. Keep the action as planned until it occurs, and record real results afterward, including mismatches. Predictions should not quietly become success claims because they seem obvious on paper.

Q14. A completed negative test

Question: Morgan is a synthetic Sales contractor and should not match Finance AND Employee. The group screen shows Morgan absent, but rule activation and relevant evaluation are unchecked. Does the unchanged display establish a completed negative test?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Not yet. Morgan should be absent, but a rule that never ran could leave the screen looking exactly the same. The display alone cannot distinguish correct exclusion from no relevant evaluation.

Establish the intended inputs, active rule, applicable processing and resulting membership. Those records show whether Morgan's absence answers the question being tested.

A negative test still needs evidence that the mechanism operated in the relevant context. 'Not in the group' becomes useful test evidence when you can connect it to that evaluation, rather than only to an unchanged screen.

Q15. One changed condition

Question: A later approved training exercise changes Casey’s department to study membership removal. The learner also considers changing the rule logic and application assignment at the same time. Why should the department comparison be observed separately first?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Simultaneous changes make it harder to explain which one produced the result. Preserve a clear before-and-after comparison around the intended input.

State the changed training requirement, predict membership, change that input, and observe the relevant processing and membership. Add another relationship as its own question later. One changed condition makes an unexpected result easier to trace without filling missing evidence with a guessed cause.

Q16. A mismatch is useful evidence

Question: An authorized training rule should include only profiles meeting Finance AND Employee. Observed membership differs from the recorded prediction, but the actual fields, exclusions, active state, and evaluation details are unchecked. Should the learner change the prediction to fit the display?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Keep the approved expectation and locate the earliest demonstrated discrepancy. Inspect actual inputs, condition, exclusions, state, processing, and membership evidence.

A mismatch can teach where the relationship differs from the plan. Rewriting the prediction hides that learning. Record the finding and next evidence needed rather than forcing a tidy success. The purpose is an explainable comparison, which remains useful even when its initial result is unexpected.

Q17. A membership result’s limits

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. All four observed memberships match the AND predictions with relevant evaluation evidence. The dedicated group has no application assignment. What result is verified, and what remains outside this experiment?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The checked profiles produced the intended membership in this rule context. That is a verified result for the defined question.

Application assignment, target accounts, entry, and business permissions were not tested. The narrow setup helps isolate membership without pretending it covers every access boundary. Report the scoped success precisely, then plan another relationship with its own prediction and evidence when the learner is ready.

Add assignment and account boundaries

Q18. A new question to test

Question: A learner has verified a synthetic user’s membership in the intended training group. A separately approved next experiment connects that group to an application. What new relationship must be predicted and checked rather than inferred from the earlier membership success?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The new question is whether the group supplies the intended user's application assignment. User-to-group membership and group-to-application assignment are separate relationships.

Record the connection and verify the actual user assignment in the intended instance. Add that boundary deliberately with its own prediction and evidence. The earlier membership result remains useful, but cannot establish a relationship the first experiment never tested.

Q19. Other assignment paths

Question: A group-based assignment experiment makes a training application visible to Casey. Direct assignments and other group paths have not been checked. Why inspect those sources before attributing the observed application relationship to the new group?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

First ask what supplied Casey's assignment. The new group is one possible path, but a direct assignment or another group could already provide it.

Compare the recorded starting state with the actual assignment source in the intended integration. A visible tile tells you where an application appears; it does not identify which relationship caused that result.

Attribute the outcome to the new group only when the relationship evidence supports it. The same distinction matters later: removing that group path would not establish that every other path ended.

Q20. Do not assume SCIM

Question: A course example uses SCIM, an interface for managing application accounts. A new authorized training application has a known owner, but its connector capabilities and account-preparation method are unchecked. May the learner assume identical automated account creation or deactivation?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Inspect the actual connector's supported and enabled behavior. The owner may prepare the target account through a separate process.

The example's contract belongs to that application; federation also does not automatically establish account creation. Decide whether account management is part of this exercise and name its supported process. A known manual responsibility is better evidence than an invented automation based on another integration.

Q21. If account management is included

Question: An approved training experiment includes updating an application account through a supported connector. The learner has prepared values in the app profile, but has not inspected the operation or target. Which evidence should the plan follow to establish the account result?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Identify the intended linked record and instance, prepared values, generated request, response, and checked target state. Interpret the response against the actual contract.

Preparation and transmission are separate observations, as are the requested state and stored result. Follow the evidence through those boundaries. Membership, assignment, or accepted user authentication cannot supply the missing account-management outcome for an operation that has not been verified.

Q22. Outside the experiment

Question: A written training plan explicitly tests only the group-to-application assignment relationship. Target-account management and application entry will be separate exercises, with no claims about them in this plan. Is excluding those boundaries necessarily an incomplete experiment?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. A limited question can have a complete answer when its conclusion remains within the stated scope. Verify the assignment relationship it actually tests.

Do not claim target creation or usable entry from that result. A later exercise can add those outcomes with appropriate owners and evidence. Completeness means answering the defined question; it does not require adding every capability to the first experiment.

Test entry and business permissions

Q23. Evidence for application entry

Question: A training sign-in exercise records an Okta success event. The application’s protocol validation, intended account selection, and protected response remain unchecked. What should the learner follow beyond the identity-service event to establish actual entry?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Start with the expected authentication requirement and evidence for this attempt. Then follow the actual protocol exchange, application validation, intended account selection, and receiving application response.

The connection determines which checks matter. One Okta event cannot stand in for the target's acceptance. Record entry separately from account state and business permissions so a later failure can be located at the boundary its evidence actually demonstrates.

Q24. Entered, but can submit?

Question: A synthetic user completes an accepted visit to a training application. The approved business goal is submitting an ordinary request, but no submission action is checked. Does verified entry establish that capability, and what test should the owner define?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Entry does not establish submission. Ask the application owner to define the appropriate action and evidence for the intended account under its approved capability.

Keep the accepted login as a success while testing the business result. A denial after entry is not automatically a password problem. The experiment's acceptance evidence must reach the action it was meant to enable rather than stopping when the first page opens.

Q25. Test the forbidden action too

Question: A training requirement allows ordinary submission but excludes approval of another person’s request. Submission works for the intended account. Why include a separate negative approval test, defined by the application owner, instead of treating the positive result as sufficient?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The negative test checks that the unapproved capability is not supplied. Successful submission would not reveal excessive approval permissions.

The owner defines the exact action and evidence for this application's implementation. Both findings matter: intended work should succeed and excluded work should not be allowed. This is a clearer acceptance test than merely asking whether the user got in or one business action worked.

Q26. No invented target test

Question: A learner plans to test a new application’s business permission using an endpoint and role copied from fictional examples in another system. The new application’s actual contract is unknown. Why should its owner supply the operation and expected result first?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The examples do not establish the new application's supported operations or permission model. Ask its owner to identify the intended capability and observable result.

Keep account state, authentication proof, and business permission evidence separate. Apply the investigation method to the actual environment rather than assuming copied request shapes work everywhere. A familiar example helps form questions; it does not supply another application's implementation contract.

Define the required end state

Q27. Plan how access ends

Question: An approved training exercise may introduce group membership, application assignments, a target account, and a local session. The learner is planning before execution. What should the end-condition section name so cleanup can be verified within the actual scope?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Name the paths to remove, required target-account state where applicable, sessions that must end, responsible owners, and observations demonstrating each outcome.

Match the cleanup scope to what the exercise introduces. A membership-only test differs from an application-access experiment. Planning the end condition beforehand makes removal an agreed, verifiable requirement rather than a vague instruction to undo everything after results have already changed.

Q28. Restore configuration versus clean effects

Question: After an approved training experiment, the learner restores the original group rule. Assignments, account operations, roles, and sessions produced during the exercise have not been inspected. Does restored configuration demonstrate that all effects were cleaned up?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Restoring the original rule answers a configuration question. It does not show what happened to assignments, target accounts, roles or sessions produced while the experiment ran.

Compare the actual changes with the agreed end condition. Identify which relationships need removal or restoration, involve their owners, and verify the required target results. Keep legitimate records and access within their approved scope.

Report the restored configuration as one completed result. Report cleanup as complete only when its relevant observations support it; a correct-looking rule cannot supply those missing application findings.

Q29. Test the old session

Question: An approved cleanup requires ending application session E. A later protected request might reuse E or follow a new sign-in creating another session. What evidence should the learner distinguish to verify termination rather than infer it from a prompt or old page?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Check whether old E still authorizes the new protected request. Distinguish that from a later sign-in establishing a different session.

A displayed prompt or previously visible page cannot alone decide the old session's authority. Use the receiving application's relevant request and session evidence. Keep identity, session, termination action, and result correlated so the cleanup conclusion answers the requirement instead of a different later access question.

Q30. Evidence and retention

Question: Several accounts and records were involved in a training experiment. Ownership or retention requirements for some are unresolved, and evidence must be kept for review. Is deleting every training-related object the default way to demonstrate cleanup?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Establish which records are involved and what the approved retention and cleanup requirement permits. Deletion cannot replace a missing ownership decision.

Keep the evidence needed to explain the exercise while excluding credentials and unnecessary personal data. Synthetic data still needs a clear record of what happened. Verify the agreed end state rather than treating cleanup as disappearance of every visible object regardless of its purpose.

Write plans and scoped conclusions

Q31. What the written record connects

Question: A learner wants an owner to review a proposed identity training experiment before execution. No configuration or target outcome has been tested yet. What should the written plan connect so its purpose, expected results, and cleanup responsibilities are clear?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Connect the question, authorized environment, synthetic population, approved outcome, starting state, predictions, intended actions, evidence, owners, and recovery or cleanup requirements.

Keep planned actions distinct from performed ones. The owner should understand what counts as success and what must happen afterward. This record gives the experiment reviewable boundaries without claiming any configuration or target result before observations establish it.

Q32. No tenant available

Question: A learner has no training tenant but can analyze synthetic profiles and a proposed group condition. Can they still create useful predictions and evidence requests, and how should the actual-result column describe work that has not been executed?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Yes. You can predict which synthetic profiles should match, identify the evidence needed to check that prediction, and name the owners and cleanup responsibilities without executing the experiment.

Keep the actual-result column as Not tested. A convincing prediction is still a statement about what should happen, not a record of what did happen.

If an authorized training environment becomes available later, add the actual observations beside the predictions, including mismatches and unfinished outcomes. The written plan remains useful preparation without being described as a tested configuration.

Q33. Separate training from Northbridge tickets

Question: Casey’s group membership succeeds in a separate synthetic training environment. In an independent business environment, Maya has an uncorrected department mapping and Jordan has retained application access. Does the training success resolve either business incident?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The people, environments, and questions are separate. The training exercise supplies no corrective outcome for those business incidents.

Apply the same evidence habits without borrowing results across cases. Casey's successful membership concerns its own inputs and observed relationship. Keep purpose and environment beside every conclusion so familiar concepts do not make independent experiments appear to share a production history.

Q34. An OR prediction is not an action

Question: Four synthetic training profiles are Casey Finance/Employee, Morgan Sales/Contractor, Riley Finance/Contractor, and Taylor Sales/Employee. The intended group condition is department Finance AND workerType Employee, with no contractor exception. A written table also includes hypothetical Finance OR Employee predictions to illustrate an error. No incorrect-rule activation is approved. Must the learner activate OR to complete this planned comparison?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The OR column illustrates why Riley and Taylor are useful boundary cases. It is a prediction comparison rather than an instruction to activate an inappropriate condition.

The intended authorized experiment uses AND. Actual results remain Not tested until observations are obtained. Keeping predictions distinct from actions allows the logic example to teach the difference without turning a hypothetical mistake into a configuration requirement.

Q35. A useful outcome report

Question: The active training rule and relevant evaluation produce the predicted memberships for four checked synthetic profiles in LAB-Finance-Employees. Application assignment, target accounts, entry, and permissions are explicitly outside the exercise. Write a useful outcome report without overstating the result.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Try: “The checked profiles produced the predicted membership under the active rule and relevant evaluation. This exercise verified LAB-Finance-Employees membership only. Application assignment, target accounts, entry, and permissions were not tested.”

That is a complete conclusion for the defined experiment. It names the evidence and scope. A next exercise can add one relationship with its own prediction, responsible owner, and verification plan.
