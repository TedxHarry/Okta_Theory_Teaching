Day 1: Questions

People, records, and the right account

Q01. One person, several records

Question: Maya is an employee. Her company uses Workday for HR information, Active Directory (AD) for directory accounts, Okta for identity and access management, and Salesforce for customer-management work. Each system has a record representing her. Why can one person need several records?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Think of HR opening Maya's employment record and the Sales team opening Salesforce. They need different information for different work. Workday records her employment details; AD holds a workplace directory account; Okta represents her for identity and access decisions; Salesforce holds the account she uses there.

The records all refer to Maya. Having several records does not create several people. Each system keeps the representation it needs.

That is why 'her account exists' needs a system name beside it. Finding her Workday record tells you about HR's record. You would still check Salesforce to find out whether her application account is ready.

Q02. Identity and account

Question: Maya is an employee identified by her name and employee number. She also has a Salesforce account for using that business application. The account is later disabled, but Maya remains employed. How does this illustrate the difference between an identity and an account?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

An identity represents who someone is. An account is a record in a particular system through which that person may sign in or receive access.

Maya's name and employee number help represent her identity. Her Salesforce account represents her in Salesforce and has its own state and permissions. Disabling it changes that application's account state, not the existence of Maya as a person.

This is why an account investigation should name the system. A problem with one representation does not automatically mean every other record or access path changed.

Q03. An account exists

Question: Salesforce is a business application that keeps a separate account for each user. A support analyst finds an account belonging to employee Maya but does not inspect its state, test sign-in, or check permissions. Which conclusion is supported: the account exists, it is enabled, or Maya can perform her required work?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Only existence is established. Finding the account tells us the record is there; it does not tell us whether it is enabled or usable.

Imagine a disabled account. It can still appear in a search while refusing access. Even an enabled account needs a successful sign-in and the permissions for Maya's particular work.

Treat existence, state, and usable access as separate checks. That lets you describe progress accurately instead of asking one successful lookup to prove several outcomes.

Q04. Two different account states

Question: A company uses Okta to manage identities and Salesforce as a separate business application. Maya’s Okta user is Active, but her Salesforce account is disabled. Are those states contradictory, and what should support clarify before changing either?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

They can both be true because they describe different records. Active concerns Maya's Okta account; disabled concerns her account inside Salesforce.

Next, establish whether Maya is approved to use Salesforce now and which owner or process manages that account state. A disabled account may need a justified correction, but the two labels alone do not explain why it was disabled.

The goal is the appropriate state in each system. Changing a correct Okta record just to make the labels agree would not answer the Salesforce requirement.

Q05. Do the identifiers have to match?

Question: Maya’s company identifies her employment record with employee number NB-1042, her Okta identity with a login, and her Salesforce account with an application username. Must those three identifiers contain identical text to represent the same person?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. NB-1042 can identify Maya's employment record while a different login or username identifies her in another system. The text does not have to be identical.

Imagine looking up a person by employee number in HR and by username in an application. Both searches can lead to the same person if the company has established which record belongs to them. 'Approved value' means the identifier the company expects for that particular lookup or connection.

Before choosing an account, check the intended system, the identifier it uses, and evidence that the record belongs to Maya. Different text is not automatically a mistake, and a familiar-looking email address is not enough to prove a match.

Q06. A familiar display name

Question: A support analyst must find employee Maya Rao’s account in a business application. The search returns two records displaying “Maya Rao,” but account ownership has not been verified. Is it appropriate to select the first result? What evidence is needed?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A display name is a useful search clue, not a verified account association. People can share a name, and records in different environments can look alike.

Establish the intended application environment, its approved user identifier, and evidence of who owns each candidate record. Those facts should support the relationship before a state or permission change is made.

Choosing the first familiar-looking result could affect another person's account. The next useful step is to identify the right record, not to guess which result the ticket meant.

Q07. The wrong environment

Question: Maya needs access to the company’s live Salesforce environment, called production. Support searches a separate Salesforce test environment and finds no account under her expected test identifier. What does that result establish about production?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It does not establish whether Maya has an account in production. It establishes only what that test-environment search found under its identifier and scope.

The next check belongs in the intended production environment using the approved production identifier. If Okta is connected to Salesforce, verify that you are inspecting the integration for that same instance.

“No match in test” is a useful scoped finding. Expanding it to “no Salesforce account exists” would discard the context needed to make the next search accurate.

Directory information and data flow

Q08. What a directory contributes

Question: A company stores employee identity records in a directory: an organized collection of user information such as names and departments. An employee’s record is present there. What does a directory contribute, and does that record alone make every business application usable?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The directory gives systems an organized place to hold and use identity information. That data can support decisions about who should receive particular access.

Storing the record does not complete every access outcome. An application assignment connects a user to an application, account management prepares the target account, authentication checks sign-in, and permissions govern actions.

Those jobs can use directory data, but their results still need verification. A record being present is the starting point for following the relationships, not proof that every application works.

Q09. Attribute or profile?

Question: Maya’s Okta profile contains firstName Maya, employeeNumber NB-1042, department Sales, and workerType Employee. A profile is a collection of user details. Which named details are attributes, and which parts are their values?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Each named detail is an attribute. Department is an attribute, and Sales is its value; workerType is another attribute, with Employee as its value. The collection of those details is Maya's profile.

This vocabulary helps you make a precise observation. “Her profile is wrong” leaves the affected field unclear. “Her Okta department is Sales” identifies the record, field, and observed value.

That statement is easier to compare with another system and avoids changing unrelated information when only one detail needs attention.

Q10. A correct value at the source

Question: Workday is the company’s HR system, and Salesforce is a separate business application with its own user records. Support confirms that Maya’s approved department is Sales and Workday stores Sales. No Salesforce department check has been made. Can support report that Salesforce also stores Sales?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The Workday observation establishes the HR value, not the Salesforce value. To report what Salesforce stores, inspect the intended Salesforce account.

Its department might be correct, stale, or maintained through a different process. Those are possibilities, not findings supplied by this question.

Compare the relevant records first. If a discrepancy appears, investigate how the information reaches the destination. A correct source is useful evidence, but it cannot provide a result from a record nobody has checked.

Q11. A system changes roles

Question: A company sends HR information from Workday into Okta, then sends application-specific information from Okta toward a business application. In each relationship, which system is the source and which is the target?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Source means where this information comes from; target means where it is intended to arrive. In Workday-to-Okta, Workday is the source and Okta the target. In Okta-to-application, Okta is the source and the application the target.

These labels describe a particular direction, not a permanent identity for a product. Okta can receive data in one relationship and supply it in another.

Name both sides before comparing results. That helps you investigate the correct boundary.

Q12. A configured connection

Question: An integration (a configured connection for supported tasks) links the company’s Workday HR system to Okta. It is enabled. Maya’s department recently changed in Workday, but support has not checked the update result or her current Okta profile. Is the enabled integration proof the latest value arrived?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. The connection identifies a way the information can move. This person's latest update is a separate result to verify.

Check Maya's current destination value and the relevant update evidence if it differs from the approved source. The integration's existence does not show that this particular change was processed.

Keep two questions apart: how is information intended to move, and what happened to this update? The first locates the investigation; the second needs actual observations.

Q13. The contractor’s record

Question: At a company, employee Maya’s HR information comes from Workday. Contractor Priya’s profile is instead maintained in Okta by an authorized administrator after her sponsor approves it. Both work with Sales. Must Priya also have a Workday record just because Maya does?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. This question supplies different maintenance processes for employees and contractors. Sharing a department does not make their source relationships identical.

For Priya, follow her approved Okta-maintenance process and the records relevant to her request. Her sponsor's approval is a business decision, not another software integration.

That approval also does not automatically authorize every application. Identify the person, applicable maintenance model, and particular access requirement rather than copying Maya's employee path.

Identity checks and action permissions

Q14. Recognized, but not permitted

Question: A user successfully signs in to a business application and reaches their account. When they try to approve another person’s expense, the application refuses. The approval requirement and role have not been checked. How does this illustrate authentication versus authorization?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Authentication concerns who is signing in. Authorization concerns what that person is allowed to do. Here, entry succeeded but an action was refused.

Check whether the person is approved to perform that action and what permission the application actually grants. The refusal alone does not tell you whether a role is incorrectly missing or the restriction is intended.

Sign-in success is useful because it locates the problem beyond entry. Recognizing a person does not automatically permit every action.

Q15. Daniel’s job title

Question: Expense is a company application for submitting and approving expenses. Daniel, a Finance manager, can sign in but receives a denial when approving an expense. No approval decision or configured approval role has been supplied. Does his job title alone justify granting the role?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The title is relevant business information, but it is not proof of an approved application capability. Confirm the Finance owner's requirement and Daniel's actual Expense permissions before proposing a grant.

This question establishes successful entry and a denied action. A password reset addresses sign-in credentials, not the permission needed for approval.

Do not invent authority from the title or grant a broad role just to remove the denial. The appropriate correction depends on the approved capability and application evidence.

Q16. Two access complaints

Question: Maya signs in to Okta, the company’s identity service, but cannot enter Salesforce; her Salesforce assignment and account have not been checked. Daniel enters a separate Expense application but cannot approve an item; his approval requirement is unknown. Why do these reports need different next checks?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Maya's report leaves application entry and account facts unresolved. Daniel's report already establishes entry and places the problem at a specific action.

For Maya, inspect the intended Salesforce assignment and target account, then the relevant sign-in result. For Daniel, establish the approval requirement and inspect his application permission and denial.

Both are access complaints, but that phrase does not name one universal failure. Locate what worked and where the requested outcome remains unverified before choosing a correction.

Q17. Clarify the requested outcome

Question: A support ticket says only “Give Maya access.” It names no application instance, action, or approval. What should support clarify before changing an account or permission?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Ask which application and environment Maya needs, what she must do there, and who approves that capability. Entering an application, viewing records, and performing a restricted action are different outcomes.

She may already have the intended account and need only an approved permission, or an account may still need preparation. The vague request does not choose between those conditions.

A clear requirement supplies a test for success. It also keeps a configuration change from becoming an access decision nobody actually authorized.

Sign-in and account management

Q18. SSO and provisioning

Question: Maya needs a Salesforce account and a way to sign in to it through the company’s Okta identity service. How do provisioning and single sign-on (SSO) address those different needs?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Start with two ordinary questions: 'Does Maya have the right Salesforce account?' and 'Can she sign in to it through Okta?' Provisioning helps create or maintain the account. SSO supports the connected sign-in.

Imagine the Salesforce owner has prepared her account, but nobody has checked her sign-in through Okta. The account work can be complete while sign-in still needs checking. Now reverse it: setting up the sign-in connection does not show that anyone created Maya's account.

For Maya to use Salesforce, follow both results. Check the intended account and its state, then check whether Salesforce accepts her actual sign-in. Permissions for her work remain another application decision.

Q19. SSO with manually prepared accounts

Question: A business application uses single sign-on through Okta, but its owner creates user accounts manually rather than through an automated connector. Is that design necessarily broken, and where would support investigate a missing account?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It can be a valid design. Single sign-on supports the sign-in relationship; the owner supplies accounts through a separate manual process.

For a missing account, request the owner's preparation result and inspect the intended target record. Do not diagnose a failed automated provisioning operation when no such mechanism is supplied.

Judge the arrangement against its requirement and results. The user needs the right account through the expected process and an accepted sign-in, even though those tasks use different mechanisms.

Q20. An update to an existing account

Question: A configured account-management process updates department on an existing business-application account. It creates no new account. Can this still be provisioning, and what result should be checked?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Yes. Provisioning includes maintaining an existing account, not only creating one. Here the intended work is an information update.

Check the relevant update response and the intended account's stored department. Those observations establish what was attempted and what the destination holds.

The account may exist and even allow sign-in while the update remains incomplete. Each result belongs to its operation, so successful entry cannot substitute for evidence that the department change arrived.

Q21. Disabling is not always deleting

Question: A company must remove a user’s access to a business application. The application supports retaining accounts in a disabled state. Must deprovisioning delete the record?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. Deprovisioning removes or disables access through the supported process. In this stated design, a retained disabled account can be the intended outcome.

Deletion removes the record; disabling changes its state. Confirm the approved requirement and verify the actual destination result rather than assuming the two actions are equivalent.

Account state also needs to be interpreted under the application's behavior. A removal request alone does not establish its completion or every access outcome.

Q22. Does SSO remove every future check?

Question: Maya’s company uses single sign-on (SSO) to connect application sign-ins with Okta. A colleague promises that this means she will never face another authentication check. Is that a reliable promise?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. SSO supports the connected sign-in relationship; it does not remove every future requirement for proving identity.

The actual experience depends on the application's connection, applicable requirements, and current context. Another authentication check can be appropriate even after an earlier successful sign-in.

Explain the benefit without an unlimited guarantee: connected applications can use the established identity relationship, while each relevant interaction still has requirements and an outcome to verify.

Assignments and visible shortcuts

Q23. Assignment without a target result

Question: Maya is assigned to the intended Salesforce integration in Okta, meaning an application-access relationship is recorded there. No target-account creation, state, or sign-in evidence has been checked. Can support report that her Salesforce account is ready?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Support can report the recorded assignment, not a ready Salesforce account. The target record and its usable state remain unverified.

An assignment may initiate configured account-management work, but initiating and completing that work are different findings. Some designs also prepare accounts through a separate owner.

Follow the actual process and inspect the intended Salesforce record. The Okta relationship answers the assignment question; it cannot supply a destination result that has not been observed.

Q24. A visible tile

Question: Maya sees a Salesforce tile (a clickable shortcut) on her Okta dashboard. No Salesforce account, entry, or permission checks are supplied. Does the visible shortcut prove any of those three outcomes?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The tile alone proves none of them. It helps locate and launch the application, but it is not a receipt for target-account existence, accepted entry, or permissions.

Inspect the actual application relationship and relevant destination evidence. Maya could click the shortcut and still be refused by Salesforce.

A visible interface element is useful context, not the complete access result. Name what the observation shows and keep the untested application outcomes separate.

Q25. No visible tile

Question: Maya’s Okta dashboard shows no Salesforce shortcut. The application connection can hide tiles, and nobody has inspected her actual Salesforce assignment. Is assignment absence established?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No. A hidden shortcut can coexist with an assignment. The question explicitly supplies that visibility option and leaves the relationship uninspected.

Check Maya's actual assignment to the intended Salesforce integration. If it is absent, compare that finding with the approved requirement. If it is present, investigate the visible shortcut and reported application outcome separately.

The missing tile is a symptom. It cannot answer the relationship question merely because it is the first thing Maya notices.

Q26. Select the supported findings

Question: Okta is the company’s identity service and Salesforce its business application. Support confirms Maya’s Okta account is Active and Salesforce is assigned in Okta. Nothing is supplied about her Salesforce account or use. Select every supported claim: A. Okta account Active. B. Salesforce assigned. C. Salesforce account enabled. D. Salesforce sign-in succeeded. E. Required Salesforce permissions ready.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A and B are supported. C, D, and E need evidence from the application account, sign-in, and relevant permission checks.

The expected process may continue from assignment, but expectation is not completion. Each claim must match an observation actually supplied.

A useful update says the Okta state and assignment are confirmed while the target outcomes remain unverified. That describes real progress without turning two checked facts into five assumed successes.

Read the evidence carefully

Q27. Finding, hypothesis, or missing evidence?

Question: Maya’s checked Okta sign-in succeeded. In the intended Salesforce environment, support found no account matching her approved identifier. Her Salesforce assignment and account-management history have not been inspected. Classify these statements: A. The checked Okta sign-in succeeded. B. Salesforce assignment is missing. C. An account under another identifier may exist. D. Account-management history is uninspected.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

A is a confirmed finding: the checked Okta sign-in worked. D tells us what nobody has inspected yet. C is a hypothesis, meaning a possible explanation to investigate. B goes too far: we cannot call the assignment missing when nobody has checked it.

The account search and the assignment check are different pieces of work. Support searched Salesforce and found no account under the expected identifier. Support has not yet inspected the assignment relationship in Okta. A negative result from the first check cannot fill in the missing result of the second.

You could write: 'Okta sign-in succeeded. The Salesforce search found no expected match. Assignment is still unknown, and an account under another identifier is one possibility to investigate.' That keeps the useful findings without turning a possibility into a cause.

Q28. What a no-match result means

Question: Support searches the intended Salesforce environment using employee Maya’s approved application identifier and finds no matching account. No other identifier or environment has been searched. Is the precise finding “no match within this check” or “no Salesforce account exists anywhere”?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The precise finding is no match within this check. Its environment and identifier scope are part of the result.

An account might exist under another identifier or elsewhere, but neither possibility is confirmed. Obtain evidence of the intended record relationship before claiming more.

The scoped result is still useful. It tells the next investigator exactly which expected match was not found. Narrow evidence can justify another check without proving an unlimited absence.

Q29. A possible identifier mismatch

Question: A search of the intended Salesforce environment finds no account under Maya’s approved identifier. Account ownership and other identifiers have not been checked. Does the search prove an identifier mismatch, and what evidence would support that explanation?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It makes a mismatch one possible explanation, not a confirmed cause. A missing account or incomplete preparation could also fit the supplied result.

To support a mismatch, find a record reliably shown to belong to Maya, establish its actual identifier, and compare it with the approved value for the intended relationship. A similar display name is not enough.

The comparison could demonstrate a specific discrepancy. Until then, keep it as a hypothesis and request evidence that distinguishes alternatives.

Q30. Choose the next evidence

Question: Maya is approved to use production Salesforce. Her checked Okta sign-in works, but Salesforce entry is not established. The production search finds no account under her approved identifier; assignment and account-management history are unknown. Name two focused evidence requests and what each would answer.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Ask first, 'Can you show Maya's assignment to the production Salesforce integration in Okta?' That answers whether the expected application relationship is actually recorded. We currently have no result for that check.

Then ask, 'Who or what prepares accounts in this Salesforce environment, and what happened to Maya's account request?' If an automated connection does the work, inspect its relevant result. If an application owner creates accounts manually, ask for that preparation record instead.

These requests give us a way forward. If the approved assignment is absent, we have found one gap to address. If it is present, we still need to understand the account search: was preparation completed, and could an existing account use another identifier? Check the intended account before creating a new one.

Q31. Evaluate the password reset

Question: Maya’s checked Okta sign-in succeeded. In the intended Salesforce environment, support found no account matching her approved identifier. Her Salesforce assignment and account-management history have not been inspected. A colleague proposes resetting her Okta password to fix the Salesforce complaint. Does the evidence justify that correction?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The checked Okta sign-in already succeeded. The unanswered questions concern the Salesforce relationship and account, not a demonstrated Okta credential failure.

A password reset changes credentials without explaining the scoped no-match result or establishing account preparation. Follow the assignment and account-management evidence first.

A later separate credential finding could call for another action. It is not supplied here. Choose a correction that follows the observed gap rather than use the same reset for every access complaint.

Q32. Should Alex create another account?

Question: Maya’s checked Okta sign-in succeeded. In the intended Salesforce environment, support found no account matching her approved identifier. Her Salesforce assignment and account-management history have not been inspected. Why is creating a new Salesforce account immediately premature?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The search did not find the expected match, but it did not establish that no existing account belongs to Maya. The intended preparation process and assignment are still unknown.

Creating another account could duplicate an existing record or bypass the approved process. First identify the correct relationship, account-management approach, and ownership evidence.

Creation can become appropriate when the requirement and findings support it. The aim is the correct account outcome, not simply an additional record that makes one search succeed.

Choose the check for the stated situation

Q33. The account exists but is disabled

Question: Maya is an employee requesting Salesforce use. Support has verified the intended account belongs to her and is disabled. Her current access approval, the reason for disabling it, and the supported re-enabling process are not supplied. What is known, and what should be checked before enabling or creating an account?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Existence and the disabled state are established. The investigation now concerns whether access is approved and how that account's state should be maintained.

Ask why it was disabled and which owner or process can appropriately change it. Assignment and sign-in results may also need checks.

Do not assume disabled means broken. It may reflect an intended decision. A second account would also address the wrong question when the correct existing record is already identified.

Q34. An absent assignment is now confirmed

Question: The approved requirement says Maya should receive production Salesforce. Support confirms her assignment to that integration is absent, but no Salesforce target-account evidence is supplied. What discrepancy is established, and what remains unverified?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The assignment is missing against the stated requirement. That supports a proposal to establish the approved relationship through its applicable process.

It does not establish whether an account exists or is enabled in Salesforce. Check expected account handling, the resulting assignment, any operation it triggers, and the intended destination record.

The unknown relationship has become a confirmed discrepancy. That makes the next action more specific while leaving the uninspected target outcome open.

Q35. An existing assignment, a manual process

Question: Maya is assigned Salesforce in Okta. The Salesforce owner confirms accounts are created manually, but neither a creation record nor Maya’s target account has been checked. Can support diagnose failed automated provisioning?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

No automated operation is supplied in this design. The stated account-preparation mechanism is manual.

Ask the responsible owner whether Maya's approved account request was received and completed, then inspect the intended Salesforce account. That follows the process actually used.

Assignment is useful evidence but not the owner's preparation result. A precise update says the relationship is present and the manual target-account outcome remains unverified, instead of inventing a failed automation.

Q36. Account creation is verified

Question: Support verifies Maya’s intended Salesforce account was created and is enabled. No attempt to enter Salesforce or perform her required business action has been checked. What can be reported as complete, and what needs further evidence?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Report the confirmed account creation and enabled state. Those are meaningful completed account-management outcomes.

Application sign-in and the permissions for her required work remain unverified. A correctly prepared account can still encounter another condition when the person uses it.

Tell Maya the account preparation is confirmed and the next check concerns actual use. Keep the successful work without stretching it into a claim that every business capability is ready.

Q37. A narrowly verified success

Question: Maya’s approved task is reading Sales records in Salesforce. Support verifies the intended assignment and enabled account, then observes a new sign-in to that account and successful reading of the approved records. Does this verify her task, and does it establish every Salesforce action?

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

It verifies the checked record-reading requirement for this person, account, and attempt. The evidence covers assignment, account state, entry, and the requested action.

It does not establish unrelated capabilities, such as administrative changes or a restricted approval. Those would have their own requirements and permission evidence.

A good closure states the successful task precisely. That is more useful than either refusing to recognize any progress or claiming unlimited access from one verified action.

Explain the journey and outcome

Q38. Explain the employee journey

Question: A company uses Workday for employee HR data, Okta for identity and access management, and Salesforce for business work. Describe the intended journey for employee Maya and the separate outcomes to verify, allowing that her Salesforce account may already exist.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

Start with her approved employee information in Workday and its relevant representation in Okta. Verify the intended application assignment, the appropriate Salesforce account through its actual maintenance process, accepted application sign-in, and the permission for her work.

Those are connected responsibilities, not a mandatory account-creation order. The target might have been prepared earlier.

Explain who does what and check each relevant record or operation. The same reasoning works with automated preparation or a separate application owner.

Q39. Repair an unsupported closure

Question: Maya’s checked Okta sign-in succeeded. In the intended Salesforce environment, support found no account matching her approved identifier. Her Salesforce assignment and account-management history have not been inspected. No correction or follow-up is supplied. Rewrite this note: “Maya has no Salesforce account anywhere. Provisioning failed, so we reset her password and closed the ticket.”

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

The note invents an unlimited absence, a cause, an action, and a successful closure. The facts support a scoped search result and unanswered process questions.

Try: “Maya's checked Okta sign-in succeeded. No Salesforce account matched her approved identifier in the intended environment. Assignment and account-management history remain uninspected. Support will obtain those records and the relevant target-account evidence before proposing a correction.”

That update leaves the case open for a concrete reason. It provides the next owner with findings and evidence requests rather than unsupported certainty.

Q40. Explain the issue to Maya

Question: Maya’s checked Okta sign-in succeeded. In the intended Salesforce environment, support found no account matching her approved identifier. Her Salesforce assignment and account-management history have not been inspected. Maya asks why support is not simply resetting her password again. Give her a friendly update explaining what works and the next justified checks.

Read the teaching explanation

Explanation:

You could say: 'Your Okta sign-in worked in the attempt we checked. Salesforce keeps a separate account for you, and we have not yet found the expected match there. We are checking whether Okta assigns Salesforce to you and how your Salesforce account is prepared. Those checks will help us choose the right fix.'

If Maya asks about the password, connect the explanation to what she already experienced: 'You were able to sign in to Okta. Changing that password would not tell us whether your Salesforce account is ready.'

This gives Maya a clear update without asking her to learn investigation terminology. It explains what worked, what support is doing next, and why the proposed password reset does not address the unanswered account questions.
